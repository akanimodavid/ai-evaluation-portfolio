We have different seasons on Earth primarily because of **the tilt of Earth's rotational axis** as it orbits the Sun, not because of changes in our distance from the Sun. 

Here’s a breakdown of how it works:

### 1. The Axial Tilt
Earth’s axis (the imaginary line running from the North Pole to the South Pole) is tilted at an angle of approximately **23.5 degrees** relative to its orbital plane around the Sun. This tilt remains pointed in the same direction in space (roughly toward the North Star, Polaris) throughout the year.

### 2. Orbiting the Sun
As Earth travels around the Sun over the course of a year, this fixed tilt means that different parts of the planet receive varying amounts of direct sunlight at different times of the year:
* **Summer**: When a hemisphere is tilted *toward* the Sun, it receives more direct sunlight and experiences longer days. This concentrated solar energy results in warmer temperatures (summer).
* **Winter**: When a hemisphere is tilted *away* from the Sun, the sunlight hits it at a shallower angle and is spread over a larger area. Days are also shorter, resulting in less solar energy and cooler temperatures (winter).
* **Spring and Autumn**: These are the transitional periods when neither hemisphere is tilted directly toward or away from the Sun, resulting in more equal day and night lengths (equinoxes) and moderate temperatures.

### 3. Opposite Seasons in Different Hemispheres
Because of this tilt, the seasons in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres are always opposite. For example, when it is summer in the Northern Hemisphere (around June), it is winter in the Southern Hemisphere. Six months later (around December), the situation reverses.

### 🚫 A Common Misconception
Many people mistakenly believe that seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to or farther from the Sun during its orbit. In reality, Earth’s orbit is only slightly elliptical, and this distance change has a negligible effect on seasonal temperatures. In fact, Earth is actually *closest* to the Sun (perihelion) in early January, which is the middle of winter for the Northern Hemisphere!

### Key Milestones in the Year:
* **Summer Solstice** (around June 21): Longest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere.
* **Winter Solstice** (around December 21): Shortest day of the year in the Northern Hemisphere.
* **Vernal (Spring) Equinox** (around March 20) and **Autumnal Equinox** (around September 22): Day and night are approximately equal in length all over the world.

In short, it’s all about the **angle and duration of sunlight** hitting the Earth due to our planet’s consistent 23.5-degree tilt as it makes its yearly journey around the Sun.
